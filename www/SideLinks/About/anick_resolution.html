The Anick resolution, step by step

November 2026

The Anick resolution builds a free \(\mathbb{Z}G\)-resolution of \(\mathbb{Z}\) from a complete rewriting system for \(G\): its free generators are the “chains” of the rewriting system, and its boundary maps come from one recursion. This note works with right \(\mathbb{Z}G\)-modules (HAP's left convention is obtained by replacing \(g\) with \(g^{-1}\)) and uses the cyclic group \(C_r\) as a running example.

David Anick introduced the resolution in 1986, as a free resolution of \(k\) over an augmented algebra over a field \(k\), with chains built from the leading terms of a Gröbner basis [Anick 1986]. The construction never divides, so it works verbatim over \(\mathbb{Z}\). Over \(\mathbb{Z}\) it was obtained algebraically by Kobayashi (1990), for monoid algebras over any commutative ring, and topologically by Brown (1992), by collapsing the classifying space of a group or monoid with a complete rewriting system.

1. What we want

We want a free \(\mathbb{Z}G\)-resolution of \(\mathbb{Z}\) with a small, explicitly known basis in each degree and boundary maps that can be computed mechanically:

\[ \cdots \to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to 0 . \]

The Anick resolution gets both from one piece of extra data: a complete rewriting system for \(G\).

2. The rewriting system

Every element of \(G\) gets one chosen word, its normal form, and a set of rules rewrites any word to that normal form. A complete rewriting system is a set of rules “word \(\to\) shorter or smaller word” which, applied in any order, always ends at the normal form.

\(G\) as a monoid. A rewriting system is a monoid presentation: words are positive words in the letters, with no formal inverses, and the Anick resolution is a resolution over the monoid ring, which is \(\mathbb{Z}G\) when the monoid is \(G\) itself. For a finite group nothing is lost, since each inverse is already a positive word (\(f^{-1} = f^{r-1}\cdot w\) with \(w\) in later letters). An infinite cyclic step needs an extra inverse letter \(A\), with tips \(aA\) and \(Aa\), and each such step multiplies the generating function of the ranks by \((1+t)/(1-t)\) instead of \(1/(1-t)\).

For a pc group with pcgs \(f_1,\dots,f_k\) and relative orders \(r_1,\dots,r_k\), collection gives exactly this:

  • Normal forms: \(f_1^{a_1} f_2^{a_2} \cdots f_k^{a_k}\), with \(0 \le a_i \lt r_i\).
  • Power rules: \(f_i^{r_i} \to\) a word in \(f_{i+1},\dots,f_k\).
  • Conjugation rules: \(f_j f_i \to f_i \cdots\) for \(j > i\), so letters out of order move left.

The key objects are the tips: the shortest words that are not normal forms. For a pc group they are \(f_i^{r_i}\) and \(f_j f_i\) with \(j > i\). Everything else is built from the tips.

3. Chains: the free generators

The basis of \(P_n\) is the set of \(n\)-chains, words built by chaining tips:

  • 0-chain: the empty word, so \(P_0 = \mathbb{Z}G\).
  • 1-chains: the letters \(f_1,\dots,f_k\).
  • 2-chains: the tips.
  • \(n\)-chains: each new tip starts inside the previous segment and ends in the new one, overlapping as little as possible.

Overlaps. An \(n\)-chain is a word covered by \(n-1\) tips, each overlapping the next, and each starting inside the segment before. For example, take \(C_2 \times C_2\) with pcgs \(f_1 = a\), \(f_2 = b\). The tips are \(aa\), \(bb\) and \(ba\) (\(ab\) is a normal form).

  • 2-chains: the tips \(aa\), \(bb\), \(ba\).
  • A 3-chain: \(b \mid a \mid a\). Here \(ba\) covers the first two segments, and \(aa\) starts at the second segment and ends in the third.
  • The rest: \(a \mid a \mid a\), \(b \mid b \mid a\) and \(b \mid b \mid b\), so \(4 = \binom{3}{1}\) in degree 3.
  • Not a chain: \(a \mid b\), because \(ab\) is a normal form, not a tip. That is why the letter indices never increase.

For a pc group an \(n\)-chain is a non-increasing sequence of letter indices \(j_1 \ge j_2 \ge \cdots \ge j_n\). Repeats of a letter alternate between the segments \(f\) and \(f^{r-1}\), because consecutive copies of the tip \(f^r\) overlap. So \(\operatorname{rank} P_n = \binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\).

Example, \(C_r\) with generator \(f\). There is one tip, \(f^r\), and one \(n\)-chain in each degree:

\[ f, \qquad f \mid f^{r-1}, \qquad f \mid f^{r-1} \mid f, \qquad f \mid f^{r-1} \mid f \mid f^{r-1}, \qquad \dots \]

So \(\operatorname{rank} P_n = 1\) for every \(n\), as for the familiar periodic resolution of \(C_r\).

4. The boundary maps

Write an \((n+1)\)-chain as \(c\cdot t\), where \(c\) is its first \(n\) segments and \(t\) its last segment, a group element. The boundary is a forced leading term minus one correction:

\[ d(c\cdot t) \;=\; c\otimes t \;-\; h\bigl(d(c)\cdot t\bigr). \]
  • Leading term \(c\otimes t\): “drop the last segment”.
  • Correction \(h(d(c)\cdot t)\): makes \(d\circ d = 0\) and exactness hold; \(h\) is a contracting homotopy defined in lower degrees.

The homotopy \(h\) is a normal-form algorithm lifted to chains. To compute \(h(c\otimes g)\), write \(g\) in normal form and ask whether \(c\) followed by the beginning \(s\) of \(\mathrm{nf}(g)\) is again a chain \(cs\):

  • If not: \(h(c\otimes g) = 0\).
  • If so, with \(g = s\cdot u\): \[ h(c\otimes g) \;=\; (cs)\otimes u \;-\; h\bigl(d(cs)\cdot u \;-\; c\otimes g\bigr). \]

The second term is the error made by the leading term, corrected again recursively. The recursion terminates because the rewriting system is Noetherian, and results are remembered on the monomials \(c\otimes g\).

Example, \(C_r\) again.

  1. \(d_1(f) = () \otimes f - () \otimes 1\), i.e. \(f - 1\).
  2. The homotopy on \(P_0\): \(h\bigl(()\otimes f^m\bigr) = \sum_{i \lt m} f\otimes f^i\). The word \(f^m\) starts with \(f\), and the recursion peels off one \(f\) at a time.
  3. \(d_2(f \mid f^{r-1}) = f\otimes f^{r-1} - h\bigl(()\otimes f^r - ()\otimes f^{r-1}\bigr)\). Since \(f^r = 1\) and \(h(()\otimes 1) = 0\), this is \(f\otimes\bigl(f^{r-1} + \cdots + f + 1\bigr) = f\otimes N\), the norm element.

So the recursion reproduces the standard periodic resolution of \(C_r\), with boundaries alternating \(f-1\) and \(N\). For a general pc group the cyclic pieces are glued together through the conjugation tips \(f_j f_i\), and that gluing produces the correction terms.

5. Why it is a resolution

The Anick resolution is a Morse complex of the bar resolution, so it is exact and inherits a contracting homotopy.

The bar resolution has basis \([g_1 \mid \cdots \mid g_n]\) for all \(n\)-tuples of nontrivial elements, far too big. The rewriting system pairs up these basis elements, by splitting or merging entries where a tip occurs. This pairing is an acyclic matching in the sense of algebraic discrete Morse theory. The unpaired, critical, basis elements are exactly the Anick chains, and the Morse complex built on them is the Anick resolution (Brown 1992; Sköldberg 2006; Jöllenbeck–Welker 2009).

A Morse complex is a deformation retract of the original complex. So the Anick resolution is homotopy equivalent to the bar resolution, hence exact, and its contracting homotopy is \(h\).

What Morse theory adds. Not the contracting homotopy: Anick's construction already has one. Nor comparison maps with the bar resolution: two contracting homotopies give those by lifting, and Crainic's version of the perturbation lemma accepts them without a strict retraction. What it adds is a clean derivation, with exactness for free, and one framework that also covers later reductions such as cancelling unit pivots, which is itself algebraic Morse theory.

6. How it relates to ResolutionNilpotentGroup

For a pc group, the ranks \(\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\) are the same as those of the twisted tensor product built step by step along the series, i.e. ResolutionNilpotentGroup: both come from the same pcgs. The Anick construction gets there by one recursion with one correction term per generator, instead of a sum of perturbation terms \(d_1 + d_2 + \cdots\). That is why it is cheaper to compute.

7. Why polycyclic groups: \(A_5\) as a contrast

Any complete rewriting system gives an Anick resolution, but only for pc groups is its size guaranteed to grow polynomially. For a pc group the ranks are \(\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\), a polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(k-1\). For \(A_5\) they grow exponentially.

Resolution of \(A_5\) Ranks in degrees 0–6
Anick, Knuth–Bendix system for \(\langle a, b \mid a^2, b^3, (ab)^5\rangle\) (17 tips) 1, 3, 17, 74, 396, 1880, 9517 (about 5× per degree)
ResolutionFiniteGroup 1, 3, 6, 10, 14, 19

The reason is visible in the growth polynomial of the normal forms, \(H(t) = \sum_{g} t^{|\mathrm{nf}(g)|}\). The Anick resolution is graded by word length, so its Euler characteristic gives Anick's identity, where \(C_n(t)\) counts \(n\)-chains by length:

\[ \sum_{n\ge 0} (-1)^n\, C_n(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{H(t)} . \]

If \(H(t)\) has a root inside the unit disk, the coefficients of \(1/H(t)\) grow exponentially, and so must the ranks.

  • Pc group: \(H(t) = \prod_i \bigl(1 + t + \cdots + t^{r_i-1}\bigr)\), whose roots are roots of unity, giving \(1/(1-t)^k\).
  • This \(A_5\) system: \(H(t)\) has coefficients 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 8, 10, 6, 3, 1 and a root of modulus about 0.50.

By Kronecker's theorem, polynomial growth is possible only if \(H(t)\) is a product of cyclotomic polynomials, i.e. the normal forms grow like those of a pc group. Whether \(A_5\) has such a complete rewriting system is open here.

Other constructions do give small, polynomially growing resolutions for \(A_5\). ResolutionFiniteGroup gives the ranks in the table above. And \(A_5\) is the even subgroup of the finite Coxeter group \(H_3 \cong A_5 \times C_2\), whose De Concini–Salvetti resolution has ranks \(\binom{k+2}{k} = 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \dots\) in degree \(k\) (HAP's ResolutionCoxeterGroup implements a version). Restricted to \(A_5\) it is a free \(\mathbb{Z}A_5\)-resolution with twice those ranks. For homology alone, HAP's GroupHomology can also work through Sylow subgroups, which are solvable.
